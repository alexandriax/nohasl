# The nohasl program map

This authored program comprises **six bands, 72 differentiated units, and eight activity types per unit: 576 lessons**. It preserves all 54 original lesson IDs and their purposes. It is a comprehensive instructional blueprint and executable learning scaffold, **not 576 reviewed video lessons**. Every unit currently has pending Deaf educator approval, missing reference signer media, and a proposed rather than validated milestone rubric.

Each unit’s eight activities are: **observe and notice; comprehension planning; grammar contrast; expressive practice; retrieval; information-gap exchange; story transfer; checkpoint**. Existing lessons occupy the matching activity type where their purpose fits, preserving previous progress. All other activities are compiled from the differentiated briefs below. English scenarios assess conceptual understanding; actual receptive ASL tasks require the missing reviewed media. Production relies on fluent examples and teacher feedback, never invented motion instructions.

The prerequisite graph is a proposed sequence, not evidence that a completed unit establishes readiness. Educator-directed placement and review may adapt it. Each unit names its immediate predecessor; earlier knowledge is revisited in new contexts. The final band is advanced and expert *practice*, not a proficiency credential or a promise that completing this map makes someone an expert.

The map draws on primary resources for language structure, culture, evaluation, variation, history, and literature. Source links in each brief support those domains; they are not endorsements, media licenses, or proof of review. ASL University cautions against reducing ASL to a single topicalized word-order formula in its [grammar discussion](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm). RIT describes a conversational assessment of function and form in its [SLPI overview](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi), while Gallaudet describes broad performance in its [ASLPI proficiency levels](https://gallaudet.edu/american-sign-language-proficiency-interview-aslpi/aslpi-preparation/aslpi-proficiency-levels/). nohasl does not administer or predict either rating.

The [Black ASL Project](https://gallaudet.edu/center-black-deaf-studies/black-asl-project/) grounds the inclusion of community language variation. Gallaudet’s [ASL literature course description](https://gallaudet.edu/asl-315-asl-literature-poetry-3/) supports studying signed works through analysis, creation, and critique. NAD’s [community and culture FAQ](https://www.nad.org/resources/american-sign-language/community-and-culture-frequently-asked-questions/) supports representing individually chosen identities and preferences. The [Clerc Center’s academic ASL standards](https://clerccenter.gallaudet.edu/resources/asl-content-standards/) are a useful separate resource for language-rich academic work; this adult learning sequence is not a claim of alignment or equivalence to those K–12 standards.

Each proposed milestone has three observable criteria. A learner can self-reflect on these criteria, but a qualified reviewer must judge linguistic accuracy and comprehensibility. A criterion should be marked **not yet evidenced**, **with support**, or **independently demonstrated in this task**; no automatic proficiency score is derived from the rubric. Unfamiliar signers, new contexts, delayed retrieval, and real partner responses are necessary to establish transfer.

## Band 1 · First connections

**Absolute beginner.** Build visual attention, legible beginnings, everyday repair, and respect for the people whose language you are learning.

### 1.01 · Hello, world

**Stable ID:** `hello-world`. **Prerequisite:** `No prerequisite; start here`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Establish shared visual attention; Open and close a brief exchange.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** greeting, acknowledgment, attention, goodbye, turn.

**Grammar:** Attention and a visible turn boundary make a greeting an exchange; an English label does not specify a complete signed utterance.

**Culture and access:** Respect someone’s attention and willingness to interact. A greeting does not create an obligation to teach.

**Receptive task:** With a fluent model, distinguish a greeting offered before attention from a greeting received and acknowledged.

**Expressive task:** Rehearse a relaxed greeting, allow a reply, and make the ending recognizable.

**Transfer scenario:** You reach a welcome desk while a volunteer is arranging name cards. **New condition:** The volunteer acknowledges you but asks for a moment before continuing.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You want to know when check-in opens; you have not greeted the volunteer yet. Partner B — You are the volunteer; check-in opens in five minutes, and you need to finish one card.

**Concept check:** What turns a greeting attempt into the start of a shared exchange? **Supported answer:** The other person has visual access and a chance to acknowledge or respond.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Completing the greeting quickly is enough even when the other person looks away. / Repeating the greeting continuously guarantees that a turn has begun.

**Milestone rubric:** Establishes attention without interrupting unnecessarily; Leaves room for acknowledgment; Signals a clear close.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/); [Primary reference 2](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm); [Primary reference 3](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-signs/h/hello.htm).

### 1.02 · Seeing clearly

**Stable ID:** `seeing-clearly`. **Prerequisite:** `hello-world`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Set up a usable signing view; Notice whole-signer information.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** face, hands, upper body, lighting, framing, comfort.

**Grammar:** Handshape, orientation, location, movement, and nonmanual features work together. Cropping any one can remove information.

**Culture and access:** Adapt the setup to the learner’s body and access needs rather than judging a single posture.

**Receptive task:** Compare two authorized views of the same utterance and identify what a face-only or hand-only crop hides.

**Expressive task:** Move within a comfortable signing space and adjust the camera so face and both hands remain visible.

**Transfer scenario:** A practice partner can see your face, but your hands disappear near the bottom of the call. **New condition:** A bright window behind you makes the hands difficult to distinguish.

**Information gap:** Partner A — Your preview looks clear at rest; ask what disappears during movement. Partner B — You can see the face but lose the non-dominant hand during the final movement.

**Concept check:** What should a usable practice view preserve? **Supported answer:** The face, both hands, and relevant upper-body movement throughout the utterance.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Only the dominant hand, because the other hand is always optional. / Only the face, because a written vocabulary label supplies the hand movement.

**Milestone rubric:** Maintains a complete comfortable frame; Identifies information lost by a crop; Adjusts the view after specific feedback.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/); [Primary reference 2](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm).

### 1.03 · Fingerspelling foundations

**Stable ID:** `fingerspelling-foundations`. **Prerequisite:** `seeing-clearly`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Distinguish letter forms in reviewed models; Practice legibility before speed.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** manual alphabet, handshape, orientation, letter, transition, dominant hand.

**Grammar:** Letters have form and orientation; J and Z involve movement. Learn the complete alphabet from reviewed visual models, not invented text descriptions.

**Culture and access:** Fingerspelling is part of ASL, not a substitute for learning its grammar and vocabulary.

**Receptive task:** Match short, educator-selected letter sequences to choices after watching the full sequence once.

**Expressive task:** Practice three personally relevant letters and their transitions using a verified alphabet model.

**Transfer scenario:** You need to spell a fictional badge code clearly to a practice partner. **New condition:** The partner understands each letter separately but loses the transition.

**Information gap:** Partner A — Your code is M-A-X; communicate it without displaying the written card. Partner B — You have M-A-X and M-A-Y badge options; ask about the final letter.

**Concept check:** What is a productive early fingerspelling goal? **Supported answer:** Clear learned letter forms with comfortable, connected transitions.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** A separate large arm movement for every letter to make spelling look emphatic. / Maximum speed before the partner can distinguish the sequence.

**Milestone rubric:** Forms selected letters from a reviewed model; Maintains useful orientation; Responds to a request to repeat a transition.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/); [Primary reference 2](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm).

### 1.04 · Names have a rhythm

**Stable ID:** `fingerspelling-in-words`. **Prerequisite:** `fingerspelling-foundations`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Recognize short words as sequences; Repair a partially understood name.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** name, word shape, rhythm, double letter, replay, clarification.

**Grammar:** Fingerspelling comprehension develops beyond isolated-letter naming. Context helps, but a plausible guess remains a guess until confirmed.

**Culture and access:** Respect people’s names. Do not replace an unfamiliar name with a more convenient one.

**Receptive task:** Watch short names from different approved signers, then identify the sequence and mark any uncertain segment.

**Expressive task:** Fingerspell a fictional name at a legible pace and repeat only the part a partner requests.

**Transfer scenario:** You meet someone named Ari but are unsure whether the final letter was I or Y. **New condition:** The next name contains a repeated letter that needs its own modeled convention.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You heard A-R and want to confirm the ending. Partner B — Your fictional name is A-R-I; clarify the ending if asked.

**Concept check:** You infer a name from context but miss its ending. What should you do? **Supported answer:** Confirm the uncertain part instead of treating your inference as fact.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Use the more common spelling because context always resolves the final letter. / Ask the person to choose a shorter name for the exercise.

**Milestone rubric:** Recognizes a short complete sequence; Identifies an uncertain segment; Repairs the segment without changing the person’s name.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/); [Primary reference 2](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm).

### 1.05 · Your first connections

**Stable ID:** `your-first-connections`. **Prerequisite:** `fingerspelling-in-words`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Introduce yourself and receive an introduction; Ask for repetition when needed.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** name, learn, meet, introduction, again, understand.

**Grammar:** Introductions have reciprocal turns. A name, a reference to self, and a question must be learned as contextual signed utterances.

**Culture and access:** Name signs carry community context; fingerspell your name rather than inventing a name sign for yourself.

**Receptive task:** Identify who is introducing whom in a short reviewed exchange; distinguish a name from a follow-up question.

**Expressive task:** Offer a greeting, share a practiced name, receive the other name, and clarify one detail.

**Transfer scenario:** At a beginner group, you and a new partner exchange names and explain that you are learning. **New condition:** Your partner has a longer name than the names in your earlier practice.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You know your own name but need the partner’s name and learning background. Partner B — You are Sam and this is your second class; ask whether your partner is new.

**Concept check:** How should a learner introduce an unfamiliar personal name? **Supported answer:** Use learned fingerspelling and clarify the name when needed.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Invent a convenient name sign without community context. / Skip the name because introductions only require a greeting.

**Milestone rubric:** Keeps the introduction reciprocal; Clarifies an unfamiliar name; Uses only names and forms actually learned.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/); [Primary reference 2](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm).

### 1.06 · Small numbers, clear meaning

**Stable ID:** `numbers-one-ten`. **Prerequisite:** `your-first-connections`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Recognize small quantities in context; Confirm a quantity without guessing.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** one to ten, quantity, how many, count, total, confirmation.

**Grammar:** Number production depends on use. Learn a quantity example separately from a sequence of digits; do not transfer orientation rules indiscriminately.

**Culture and access:** Accuracy matters more than a speed score when exchanging quantities.

**Receptive task:** Match reviewed quantity utterances to groups of objects, then distinguish quantity from an identifying digit.

**Expressive task:** Communicate quantities for three sets of classroom supplies using approved number models.

**Transfer scenario:** You and a partner divide supplies for a small table activity. **New condition:** The partner interprets the number as a table label rather than a quantity.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You have four markers and need six in total; ask for the missing quantity. Partner B — You have two spare markers and three spare pencils; clarify which supply is needed.

**Concept check:** Why establish what a number refers to? **Supported answer:** A quantity and an identifying digit can serve different communicative functions.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** The number alone always identifies the object and the purpose. / All contexts require exactly the same number production conventions.

**Milestone rubric:** Identifies the quantity and its referent; Uses an approved contextual number form; Confirms the exchanged total.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi); [Primary reference 3](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/numericalincorporation.htm).

### 1.07 · Questions that invite yes or no

**Stable ID:** `yes-no-questions`. **Prerequisite:** `numbers-one-ten`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Recognize a yes/no question in context; Coordinate a practiced question and response.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** yes, no, question, answer, ready, want.

**Grammar:** Yes/no question marking involves nonmanual grammar and its timing across the utterance; it is not an eyebrow gesture added after arbitrary English word order.

**Culture and access:** A question invites a real response, including a refusal or a request for clarification.

**Receptive task:** Compare approved statements and yes/no questions with similar vocabulary; attend to the complete face and body.

**Expressive task:** Practice a learned readiness or preference question and pause long enough for the response.

**Transfer scenario:** You want to know whether a partner is ready to begin a practice round. **New condition:** The partner answers with a clarification rather than a simple yes.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You are ready but need to check the partner before starting. Partner B — You need another minute; communicate that and confirm when to start.

**Concept check:** What must a learner study when learning a yes/no question? **Supported answer:** The complete signed question, including the timing of its nonmanual marking.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Only a final English question word added to any statement. / An isolated facial pose independent of the signed utterance.

**Milestone rubric:** Distinguishes a question from a statement; Coordinates learned nonmanual timing; Waits for and responds to the answer.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi).

### 1.08 · Ask for the missing piece

**Stable ID:** `wh-questions`. **Prerequisite:** `yes-no-questions`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Identify the information a question requests; Form a focused clarification.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** who, what, where, when, which, information.

**Grammar:** Information questions differ in purpose from yes/no questions. Learn grammatical form and nonmanual scope through complete contextual examples.

**Culture and access:** Ask only for information relevant to the exchange; curiosity does not override personal boundaries.

**Receptive task:** Match reviewed information questions to the kind of answer requested, not merely to a familiar isolated sign.

**Expressive task:** Ask one learned question about a missing location or person and follow the answer.

**Transfer scenario:** A workshop announcement mentions a meeting but you missed its location. **New condition:** The answer contains a place name you have not encountered before.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You know the meeting is today and need the room. Partner B — The room is Studio B; if asked about time, explain that time is already confirmed.

**Concept check:** Which question best repairs a missed location? **Supported answer:** A focused request for the place where the meeting will occur.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** A yes/no question about whether any meeting exists. / A request to repeat every detail even though only the location is unclear.

**Milestone rubric:** Identifies the missing information type; Asks a relevant learned question; Checks an unfamiliar answer.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi).

### 1.09 · People, objects, and reference

**Stable ID:** `pointing-and-possession`. **Prerequisite:** `wh-questions`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Establish a referent before returning to it; Distinguish a person from their possession.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** I, you, person, object, mine, yours, reference.

**Grammar:** Indexing and possessive forms have distinct functions. A location in signing space gains reference through context, not from pointing anywhere at random.

**Culture and access:** Pointing in signed discourse should not be judged by unrelated spoken-language etiquette rules.

**Receptive task:** Track which person and which belonging are referenced in an approved two-person exchange.

**Expressive task:** Introduce two people and identify one belonging of each using forms already modeled.

**Transfer scenario:** Two learners have left similar notebooks on a shared desk. **New condition:** A third notebook appears and needs a newly established referent.

**Information gap:** Partner A — Your notebook has a green sticker; ask which notebook belongs to the partner. Partner B — Your notebook has a blue sticker; the unmarked notebook belongs to the teacher.

**Concept check:** What makes a reference understandable to a partner? **Supported answer:** A clearly established person or object and consistent subsequent reference.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** A new arbitrary location each time the same person is mentioned. / Assuming pointing and possession always use the same form.

**Milestone rubric:** Establishes each referent; Keeps person and possession distinct; Maintains references when a new object appears.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi).

### 1.10 · Likes, dislikes, and a clear no

**Stable ID:** `preferences-and-negation`. **Prerequisite:** `pointing-and-possession`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Express a familiar preference; Recognize what is being negated.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** like, dislike, want, not, prefer, choice.

**Grammar:** Negation can involve manual and nonmanual elements with meaningful scope. Study what part of the utterance is negated rather than attaching a generic no to everything.

**Culture and access:** Respect a partner’s no without requiring an explanation they do not wish to give.

**Receptive task:** Identify whether a reviewed utterance rejects an item, a time, or the whole proposed activity.

**Expressive task:** Express one preference and one respectful refusal using learned contextual forms.

**Transfer scenario:** A partner offers tea before a practice meeting; you want water instead. **New condition:** The partner thinks you declined the meeting rather than the drink.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You do not want tea but would like water; make the distinction clear. Partner B — You can offer tea or water and need to know which to bring.

**Concept check:** What needs clarification when a negative response is misunderstood? **Supported answer:** Which item or proposition the negation applies to.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** That any negative response rejects the entire interaction. / That facial marking can be omitted whenever a manual sign is used.

**Milestone rubric:** Expresses an understandable preference; Makes the scope of refusal clear; Repairs a mistaken interpretation.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi).

### 1.11 · Keep the exchange going

**Stable ID:** `visual-turns-and-repair`. **Prerequisite:** `preferences-and-negation`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Recognize a turn opening and ending; Use focused repair instead of pretending.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** turn, pause, repeat, slower, clarify, confirm.

**Grammar:** Gaze, pauses, and other contextual cues help organize turns. A request for repetition can target a specific segment of an utterance.

**Culture and access:** Repair is normal language use. Give and request feedback without shaming a learner.

**Receptive task:** Observe an approved exchange with a repair and identify what was misunderstood and how it was resolved.

**Expressive task:** Rehearse a pause, a learned clarification request, and a confirmation of the new information.

**Transfer scenario:** Your partner gives a day and a location; you understand the day but miss the location. **New condition:** Repetition does not help, so a rephrased explanation is needed.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You know Tuesday; ask only about the place. Partner B — You proposed the library entrance; repeat or rephrase that part when asked.

**Concept check:** What should happen when repeating the same phrase still does not resolve a misunderstanding? **Supported answer:** Try an appropriate rephrase or another focused clarification.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Repeat faster to force the partner to adapt. / Agree without understanding to avoid interrupting the flow.

**Milestone rubric:** Locates the misunderstanding; Uses an appropriate repair strategy; Confirms shared understanding.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/); [Primary reference 2](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm).

### 1.12 · A visual language

**Stable ID:** `a-visual-language`. **Prerequisite:** `visual-turns-and-repair`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Integrate a first reciprocal introduction; Explain why ASL includes grammar and culture.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** handshape, movement, location, orientation, nonmanuals, community.

**Grammar:** ASL has its own grammatical system. The components of a sign and the organization of discourse cannot be replaced by a sequence of English labels.

**Culture and access:** Deaf identities and language histories are diverse; follow individuals’ chosen terms and communication preferences.

**Receptive task:** Follow two approved introductions by different signers and note both shared information and variation.

**Expressive task:** Complete a brief introduction, ask an unplanned question, and repair one misunderstanding with a partner.

**Transfer scenario:** At the end of a beginner workshop, you meet a new participant without a script. **New condition:** The partner uses a familiar concept with a variant you have not seen.

**Information gap:** Partner A — Find out the participant’s name and what they want to practice. Partner B — You are Lee and want to practice names; ask your partner about their goal.

**Concept check:** A sign differs from your first classroom example. What follows? **Supported answer:** Ask about its meaning and context before assuming it is incorrect.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Every differing form must be an error because a language has one form per word. / Any movement is equally valid in every context once you can guess the meaning.

**Milestone rubric:** Sustains a short reciprocal introduction; Uses repair when a variant is unfamiliar; Recognizes cultural and grammatical learning goals.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.nad.org/resources/american-sign-language/community-and-culture-frequently-asked-questions/); [Primary reference 2](https://nad.org/who-we-are/our-language/).

## Band 2 · Everyday life

**Beginner.** Connect people, places, time, quantities, and familiar routines in increasingly useful everyday exchanges.

### 2.01 · People and places

**Stable ID:** `people-and-places`. **Prerequisite:** `a-visual-language`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Introduce people important to you; Maintain references across a short description.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** friend, family, household, live, place, relationship.

**Grammar:** Introduce a referent before using a spatial reference again; maintain clear links between people and places.

**Culture and access:** Describe your actual relationships, including chosen family and varied household structures.

**Receptive task:** Map the people and places in an approved household introduction without assuming relationships that were not stated.

**Expressive task:** Introduce two people and describe where each lives using familiar structures.

**Transfer scenario:** A community group is making a fictional neighborhood welcome map. **New condition:** The two people have the same first name and need another identifying detail.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You know that Pat lives near the park and need to learn where Jo lives. Partner B — Jo lives near the library; ask which person lives near the park.

**Concept check:** What should a viewer infer from a household description? **Supported answer:** Only relationships and locations that are stated or clearly established.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** That everyone in the household has the same family relationship. / That a repeated first name always refers to the same person.

**Milestone rubric:** Introduces people clearly; Maintains person-place links; Avoids unsupported assumptions.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.nad.org/resources/american-sign-language/community-and-culture-frequently-asked-questions/); [Primary reference 2](https://nad.org/who-we-are/our-language/).

### 2.02 · Describe with care

**Stable ID:** `describing-people`. **Prerequisite:** `people-and-places`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Select identifying details that serve a purpose; Respect how a person describes themselves.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** appearance, clothing, height, identify, description, context.

**Grammar:** Descriptions need an established referent and organized relevant details. Learn the contextual use of descriptive forms from fluent examples.

**Culture and access:** Physical descriptions should be respectful and relevant; do not infer identity or character from appearance.

**Receptive task:** Choose the intended person from an approved description, distinguishing stated features from assumed ones.

**Expressive task:** Describe a fictional person using two useful visible details without value judgments.

**Transfer scenario:** A volunteer needs to find a colleague in a group wearing similar event shirts. **New condition:** One identifying accessory is removed, so you need a different relevant detail.

**Information gap:** Partner A — The colleague has a yellow scarf and a name badge; communicate useful details. Partner B — Two people wear yellow; ask which additional detail distinguishes the colleague.

**Concept check:** Two people share the first detail you gave. What should you add? **Supported answer:** Another relevant identifying feature that you actually know.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** An assumed personality trait based on appearance. / The same detail with more emphasis instead of clarifying.

**Milestone rubric:** Chooses relevant observable details; Keeps the referent clear; Responds respectfully when identification fails.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi).

### 2.03 · Home and belongings

**Stable ID:** `home-and-belongings`. **Prerequisite:** `describing-people`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Describe a familiar room; Distinguish location from ownership.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** room, table, chair, door, near, belongings.

**Grammar:** Spatial description benefits from a stable viewpoint and established landmarks; ownership and location communicate different relationships.

**Culture and access:** Do not assume one housing arrangement or standard of living when discussing home.

**Receptive task:** Reconstruct a simple room from an approved description and identify which details locate an object.

**Expressive task:** Describe a room with three landmarks and one belonging using modeled spatial language.

**Transfer scenario:** A housemate asks where a borrowed notebook was left. **New condition:** The room contains two tables and one needs a distinguishing landmark.

**Information gap:** Partner A — The notebook is on the table near the door, not on the owner’s desk. Partner B — You know the owner’s desk but need the notebook’s current location.

**Concept check:** Knowing who owns an object tells you… **Supported answer:** Its owner, but not necessarily its current location.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Exactly where it must be in the room. / That spatial landmarks are unnecessary for finding it.

**Milestone rubric:** Establishes room landmarks; Separates ownership and location; Clarifies an ambiguous object position.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-layout/grammar.htm); [Primary reference 2](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/slpi).

### 2.04 · Beyond counting on ten

**Stable ID:** `numbers-eleven-hundred`. **Prerequisite:** `home-and-belongings`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Recognize two-digit quantities; Distinguish number contexts.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** eleven to one hundred, quantity, address, digits, total, correction.

**Grammar:** Two-digit numbers and digit strings have contextual conventions. Learn quantity, address, and identifier examples separately with approved models.

**Culture and access:** Allow repetition and confirmation when an incorrect number would change a practical outcome.

**Receptive task:** Identify educator-selected two-digit quantities and address digits from reviewed recordings with unfamiliar signers.

**Expressive task:** Exchange a fictional quantity and an address, explicitly establishing each number’s purpose.

**Transfer scenario:** A group needs twenty-four chairs delivered to room forty-two. **New condition:** The partner reverses the quantity and the room number.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You know the chair quantity and need to confirm the room identifier. Partner B — You know room 42 and need to confirm that the quantity is 24, not 42.

### 4.04 · A change of perspective

### 4.08 · Why the event changed

**Expressive task:** Tell an original or permitted story and make its source, scope, and perspective clear.

**Transfer scenario:** A friend permits you to discuss a learning experience in a small class but not to post their video publicly. **New condition:** A group member asks to upload the classroom recording to a public channel.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You want to share a reflection and must stay within the friend’s stated permission. Partner B — You are the friend; clarify which details and uses are acceptable.

**Concept check:** What does permission to discuss a story in one class establish? **Supported answer:** Permission for that agreed use, with other uses requiring their own appropriate consent.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Unlimited permission to publish the person’s video and details. / Permission to change the person’s perspective while presenting it as their account.

**Milestone rubric:** States source and perspective clearly; Respects the agreed scope of use; Distinguishes adaptation from another person’s account.

**Approval:** Pending Deaf educator review. Reference signer media not yet supplied. Rubric is proposed, not validated.

**Sources:** [Primary reference 1](https://www.nad.org/resources/american-sign-language/community-and-culture-frequently-asked-questions/); [Primary reference 2](https://nad.org/who-we-are/our-language/).

### 6.10 · Beyond the prepared response

**Stable ID:** `sustained-unscripted-dialogue`. **Prerequisite:** `ethical-storytelling`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Maintain an extended spontaneous exchange; Repair complex misunderstandings flexibly.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** elaboration, spontaneous, clarify, rephrase, topic, follow-up.

**Grammar:** Sustained interaction draws on comprehension, grammatical control, elaboration, discourse management, and flexible repair across unfamiliar turns.

**Culture and access:** Work with consenting fluent partners and qualified reviewers; a conversational relationship is not an automatic assessment arrangement.

**Receptive task:** Follow extended authorized exchanges with unfamiliar signers and identify where meaning is negotiated.

**Expressive task:** Discuss a familiar and a less familiar topic with a fluent partner who asks unplanned follow-ups.

**Transfer scenario:** A practice partner asks how a project changed your opinion, then challenges one assumption. **New condition:** The partner introduces a new example that partly contradicts your explanation.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You explain your project and stay responsive rather than returning to a memorized monologue. Partner B — You ask an unexpected but relevant follow-up and request a rephrase if needed.

**Concept check:** What demonstrates flexible conversation beyond a prepared script? **Supported answer:** Meaningful comprehension and adaptation to unplanned turns, including appropriate repair.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Returning to the memorized passage whenever a new question appears. / Avoiding clarification to maintain the appearance of uninterrupted fluency.

**Milestone rubric:** Responds to unplanned information; Elaborates coherently across turns; Repairs misunderstandings without abandoning the exchange.

### 6.11 · Depth in a domain

**Stable ID:** `domain-depth`. **Prerequisite:** `sustained-unscripted-dialogue`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Develop precise language in a chosen field; Explain specialist ideas to different audiences.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** domain, terminology, definition, example, audience, limitation.

**Grammar:** Specialized discourse needs accurate concepts, contextual terminology, grammatical organization, and the ability to paraphrase when a term is unfamiliar.

**Culture and access:** Domain language learning does not confer professional competence, interpreting credentials, or permission to provide expert services.

**Receptive task:** Study authorized signed explanations from qualified people in a chosen domain and identify their definitions and examples.

**Expressive task:** Explain a concept you genuinely understand to a fluent non-specialist, then to an informed partner.

**Transfer scenario:** You explain a hobby process, such as seed storage, without assuming everyone knows its technical terms. **New condition:** A term has different meanings in two communities or professional contexts.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You understand the process and must define one specialist concept in accessible language. Partner B — You are a fluent non-specialist and ask for a concrete example of that concept.

**Concept check:** What should happen when a specialist term is unfamiliar? **Supported answer:** Clarify its meaning in context and use a relevant definition or example.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Assume fluent general ASL guarantees shared knowledge of every technical term. / Invent an authoritative sign without checking domain usage.

**Milestone rubric:** Uses accurate domain concepts; Explains unfamiliar terminology accessibly; Identifies the limits of language and professional expertise.

### 6.12 · Your next chapter

**Stable ID:** `your-next-chapter`. **Prerequisite:** `domain-depth`. **Activities:** all eight types.

**Outcomes:** Assemble varied evidence of communication; Set a reviewed plan for continued development.

**Vocabulary and concepts:** portfolio, assessment, strengths, evidence, goals, community.

**Grammar:** Broad proficiency requires performance across receptive, expressive, and interactive tasks; no single script, vocabulary total, or lesson count establishes it.

**Culture and access:** Long-term growth includes authentic relationships, Deaf-led learning, and independently appropriate assessment; course completion is not interpreter qualification.

**Receptive task:** Review varied consented samples with a qualified person and identify strengths and recurring comprehension gaps.

**Expressive task:** Complete a narrative, explanation, and unscripted interaction, then define the next goals with a qualified reviewer.

**Transfer scenario:** You present a portfolio containing a revised narrative, a domain explanation, and a spontaneous conversation. **New condition:** The learner performs well on a familiar topic but struggles with an unfamiliar signer.

**Information gap:** Partner A — You identify one strength and one uncertainty, linking each to a specific sample. Partner B — You are the qualified reviewer; request an additional task where the evidence is incomplete.

**Concept check:** What should a final program milestone establish? **Supported answer:** A documented body of task-specific evidence and a next learning plan, without automatically certifying fluency.

**Misconceptions used as distractors:** Expert proficiency solely because every activity is marked complete. / Professional interpreting qualification from a successful solo recording.

**Milestone rubric:** Presents varied authentic evidence; Distinguishes participation from assessed performance; Sets specific next goals with qualified feedback.

## Content production acceptance criteria

Before a unit becomes an instructional release, a Deaf-led team must review its language and cultural framing, supply licensed full-signer media for every receptive and production target, verify acceptable variants, annotate meaning and grammatical features, and test the activities with learners. Each concept question needs review for ambiguity and appropriate distractors. Two qualified reviewers should reconcile discrepancies; an additional signer should test receptive transfer. Access review must include body and camera diversity, captions, keyboard interaction, reduced motion, and alternatives to camera use.

Fingerspelling, numbers, depicting constructions, aspect, agreement, role shift, register, and literary forms require visual modeling. The blueprint deliberately gives no invented hand or face movement recipes. A concept question answered in English is not receptive ASL evidence, and a completed camera attempt is not evidence of correct signing. References are links to primary sources, not bundled media. Record author, reviewers, performers, rights, variant context, approval date, and content version when material is produced.

The authored source is `lib/data/program_blueprints.dart`; the activity compiler and preserved legacy lessons are in `lib/data/curriculum.dart`. All unit IDs and old lesson IDs are stable. New lesson IDs combine unit ID and activity kind. See `docs/CURRICULUM.md` for the overall pedagogy and staged release plan. Sources consulted 2026-09-05.
