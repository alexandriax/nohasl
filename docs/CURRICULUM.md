# nohasl curriculum and instructional release plan

nohasl is designed to support an absolute beginner’s progress toward sustained, nuanced communication in American Sign Language. The application contains a **six-band program with 72 authored unit briefs, eight purposeful activity types per unit, 576 lessons, and 2,250 steps**. The program spans foundations through advanced and expert practice, with structured language goals, concept questions, information gaps, stories, rehearsal, and milestone rubrics. It is a comprehensive authored program blueprint and executable learning scaffold; it is not 576 reviewed video lessons or a complete educator-approved ASL course. The [full program map](PROGRAM_MAP.md) specifies every unit’s outcomes, grammar, vocabulary and concepts, culture, receptive and expressive tasks, partner roles, transfer scenario, misconception checks, rubric, prerequisites, and sources.

**Content status: authored demo; pending Deaf educator review.** The current build has no licensed reference-signer library, no automatic sign-correctness assessment, and no verified proficiency assessment. Text scenarios do not test receptive ASL. Practice completion and XP record participation; they do not certify accuracy, fluency, readiness to interpret, or an official proficiency level. Product levels below are independent of Gallaudet’s ASLPI ratings.

ASL is a full language embedded in Deaf communities. Its grammar includes manual and nonmanual resources, and its study should include cultural context and real interaction. These principles are grounded in the [National Association of the Deaf’s description of ASL](https://nad.org/who-we-are/our-language/), [NAD guidance on learning ASL](https://nad.org/knowledge-hub/american-sign-language/learning-american-sign-language/), and [ASL University’s curriculum overview](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/curriculum/curriculum.htm). The lesson sequence, fictional scenarios, product terminology, and exercises here are original proposals; none of these organizations endorses nohasl.

## The six-level path

The path is spiral: previously encountered communication functions reappear with less scaffolding, longer turns, different signers, and more demanding contexts. Advancement ultimately requires evidence of receptive, expressive, interactive, and cultural competence. The present demo exposes the whole path so it can be explored; it does not enforce evidence-based placement.

| Level | Scope of its 12 units | Intended communicative outcome | Future performance task |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · First connections** — absolute beginner | Attention and framing; fingerspelling; introductions; small quantities; questions; reference and possession; preferences; repair; culture | Establish visual attention, greet, introduce oneself, request repair, and notice the components of signed language. | A brief reciprocal introduction with a fluent partner, including an unplanned clarification. |
| **2 · Everyday life** — beginner | People and households; respectful description; numbers and ages; routines; calendar and time; food; shopping; work; weather; café integration | Discuss familiar people, routines, and preferences; use basic reference and time frames; handle a small everyday exchange. | A café or community-space interaction with a changed option and a relevant follow-up. |
| **3 · Connecting ideas** — developing | Plans and boundaries; reasons; spatial description; entity and handling constructions; agreement; aspect; transport; services; collaborative problem solving | Negotiate plans, give useful descriptions, establish spatial relationships, and solve ordinary misunderstandings. | Plan a shared outing and explain a route while responding to partner questions. |
| **4 · Stories in motion** — intermediate | Narrative structure; time shifts; setting; perspective; constructed action and dialogue; moving scenes; causality; suspense; repair; mystery; portfolio | Maintain narrative sequence, distinguish perspectives, and integrate spatial and nonmanual resources coherently. | Retell an event with multiple characters, explain a mistaken assumption, and answer questions. |
| **5 · In the community** — upper intermediate | Group discourse; topic management; supported opinion; negotiation; identity and variation; Deaf history; access; remote and workplace interaction; event collaboration | Contribute to longer and group exchanges, discuss alternatives, adapt to variation, and participate respectfully. | Help plan and evaluate a community event with several interlocutors and changing information. |
| **6 · Beyond the familiar** — advanced and expert practice | Extended explanation; systems; hypotheses; argument; information analysis; register; literature and critique; ethical storytelling; spontaneous dialogue; domain depth; portfolio | Work toward spontaneous discussion, extended explanation, supported opinion, hypothetical reasoning, and purposeful register shifts. | A varied portfolio plus a sustained unscripted interview reviewed by qualified people. |

Advanced outcomes require sustained authentic practice, receptive work with varied signers, and qualified assessment. Each band contains 96 activities, but quantity and completion alone do not establish any proficiency level. For a useful external description of what broad conversational performance entails, see [Gallaudet’s ASLPI proficiency descriptions](https://gallaudet.edu/american-sign-language-proficiency-interview-aslpi/aslpi-preparation/aslpi-proficiency-levels/). nohasl does not administer, imitate, award, or predict an ASLPI result.

## What a production lesson should contain

Each lesson needs a measurable communication goal and a complete sequence: **notice → understand → produce → exchange → reflect → retrieve later**. Difficulty should come from the language task, not time pressure or small interface targets.

1. **A meaningful opening.** A short Deaf-performed interaction establishes why the language is useful. Captions, translations, and vocabulary hints are learner-controlled so receptive practice can occur before English is revealed.
2. **Whole-signer observation.** Normal-speed and slower playback preserve the face, both hands, torso, timing, and relevant space. A learner can loop a phrase or compare an educator-approved alternate view. Slow playback is supplemental; natural rhythm must remain available.
3. **Focused noticing.** Ask about one meaningful contrast: a referent, time shift, palm orientation, question marking, movement, or turn boundary. Distractors should diagnose specific misunderstandings and include explanations.
4. **Production in context.** A camera task asks the learner to communicate something, first with a model and then with a changed detail. The user can pause, repeat, review, or complete an equivalent off-camera practice task.
5. **An exchange.** A partner or carefully bounded signed scenario contributes information the learner did not already know. Clarification and repair are legitimate successful outcomes.
6. **Reflection and later retrieval.** Track confidence separately from teacher or validated model feedback. Revisit the communication function on another day, with a different signer, then in a new context.

The expanded program compiles eight distinct activity structures from each differentiated unit brief: observation, comprehension planning, grammar contrast, expressive camera work, retrieval, information-gap exchange, story transfer, and checkpoint. New activities have four steps; the 54 original lessons preserve their existing three-step content and IDs in the matching activity slots. Correct answer positions vary. Each checkpoint offers an ungraded self-reflection rather than an automatic pass/fail judgment. The structures are usable for guided planning and rehearsal but must not be presented as equivalent to a media-complete instructional sequence. Reference-media requirements are explicit on 574 of the 576 activities; the initial greeting and camera-framing exercise are the exceptions. A reference link is not an embedded lesson or permission to redistribute a signer’s work.

The first greeting uses a natural side-to-side wave, consistent with [ASL University’s HELLO discussion](https://www.lifeprint.com/asl101/pages-signs/h/hello.htm). Other vocabulary labels are not motion instructions. **An illustration, static hand, English gloss, or a moving decorative character is not a validated ASL model.** Release requires a reviewed demonstration for every production target.

## Activity variety and the engagement loop

The application already distinguishes five lesson modes. Their production destinations are:

| Mode | Learner action | Meaningful evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Guided** | Watch a complete example, notice a contrast, try a small variation. | Identifies the distinction and applies it in context. |
| **Camera** | Practice, compare to an approved model, revise one feature, try again. | Improvement on a specific rubric criterion, with uncertainty visible. |
| **Story** | Follow episodic signed events, identify a clue, predict, and retell. | Comprehension and narrative continuity across several signers and events. |
| **Conversation** | Exchange information, respond to a surprise, negotiate or repair. | A partner understands the intended message and the learner responds to new information. |
| **Recall** | Retrieve a previously learned form or meaning without first seeing the answer. | Retention across days, contexts, and signer variation. |

The café, missing-sketchbook mystery, and community-art-event stories create recurring settings and reasons to return. A production narrative can introduce new characters and perspectives while reusing known language. Authorship and casting should involve Deaf writers, educators, actors, and creators from the beginning.

Useful later activities include information-gap map tasks, two-sided schedule negotiation, number-in-context challenges, fingerspelling in words, role-shift detective scenes, error repair, viewpoint retellings, and collaborative event planning. A streak may celebrate consistency but should have flexible rest days. Avoid rewarding speed, discouraging necessary repetition, ranking bodies, or using a “fluency percentage” that has no validated interpretation.

## ASL, culture, and accessibility throughout

Language and culture are not separate end-of-course modules. Introductions include attention and turn-taking; description includes perspective and referents; questions include nonmanual timing; stories include role shift and constructed action; advanced work includes discourse, register, variation, and ASL literature.

People’s identity and communication preferences must be represented accurately and individually. Do not assume all Deaf people sign, prefer the same labels, or have the same experience. [NAD’s community and culture FAQ](https://www.nad.org/resources/american-sign-language/community-and-culture-frequently-asked-questions/) explains the diversity of identities and the importance of people’s chosen terms. Regional, generational, racial, and other language varieties require informed representation; a classifier trained on one form must not call all alternatives errors.

The interface should remain fully usable without sound. Optional narration, ambience, or achievement audio can support individual preferences but must never carry essential feedback alone. Every cue has a visual equivalent; provide captions, reduced motion, keyboard and switch access, visible focus, adjustable text, and usable contrast. Layouts must preserve full signing video rather than cropping hands or face for visual style. Comfort and mobility adaptations need educator input rather than a single idealized posture.

DeafBlind learners and learners with different motor or visual access needs require their own researched accommodations and potentially distinct instructional pathways. Do not promise that a visual camera course already serves every access need.

## Reference-media and content production requirements

A reviewed release needs a media and content manifest for every instructional target. At minimum record:

- Stable concept and lesson IDs, prerequisites, communication function, level, and review dates.
- Deaf instructional author, fluent performer, independent reviewers, compensation, usage rights, release permissions, and attribution.
- Intended meaning, context, regional or community variant labels, grammatical features, likely confusions, and acceptable alternatives.
- Full-signer videos with natural-speed originals, approved crops or alternate angles, timing annotations, accessible translations, and poster frames.
- A task rubric distinguishing lexical form, manual and nonmanual grammar, comprehensibility, discourse, and repair; specify when a criterion is not applicable.
- A modality tag for every step: receptive video, expressive production, interactive exchange, cultural learning, concept check, or reflection.

Before release, two qualified reviewers should independently inspect each example and task, reconcile differences, and test with learners. A Deaf-led editorial group should approve contentious terminology, variants, cultural scenarios, and representation. Confirm media rights before distribution. External links are learning references, not a license to download or bundle videos.

The [Gallaudet Clerc Center’s learning-ASL resource guidance](https://clerccenter.gallaudet.edu/ndec/educational-resources/learning-asl/) emphasizes classes and interaction with Deaf signers. The product should help learners reach such opportunities. It should not imply that community members are an unpaid on-demand correction service.

## Camera feedback without overstating what a model knows

The current experience is self-review. Framing, consent, and camera availability are distinct from learning assessment. Future capability should progress in explicit stages:

1. **Capture quality:** detect missing hands, occlusion, poor lighting, or an incomplete frame, with opt-in, accessible explanations. A good frame means the video is visible, not that the ASL is correct.
2. **Bounded form feedback:** compare a small licensed set of isolated targets, report uncertainty, and abstain when the signer, variant, or capture is outside validated conditions.
3. **Phrase-level review:** assess movement over time alongside face, body, and context. Lexical recognition alone does not establish grammatical or pragmatic correctness.
4. **Teacher-assisted interpretation:** route difficult cases and longer discourse to qualified human feedback. Explain the distinction between automated observations and educator judgments.

Evaluate separately across signers, skin tones, handedness, camera angles, devices, clothing, backgrounds, signing variation, and relevant motor differences. Prevent signer identity leakage across training and evaluation sets. Publish target-specific error patterns and abstention rates before making claims. User video is not training data by default; explicit consent, deletion, retention, and sharing controls belong in the architecture.

## Evidence, placement, and next releases

The program contains 324 indexed concept checks, including deliberate retrieval of earlier distinctions, and 72 ungraded checkpoint reflections. These assess or reflect on reading, concepts, and the learner’s own evidence. They do not observe receptive ASL comprehension or establish signing quality. Each of the 72 units has a specific receptive task, expressive task, authentic scenario, partner information gap, transfer twist, and three observable milestone criteria. Completion remains an honest participation record, separate from a qualified assessment of a performance sample.

For an instructional pilot, scope the release to reviewed foundational material. Collect baseline, delayed-recall, receptive-comprehension, and teacher-scored expressive samples. Include unseen signers and new contexts; use consented, compensated participant studies. Examine whether learners transfer what they practiced to real conversation. Review confusion reports and false model feedback as content issues, not just analytics.

Only then expand one reviewed level at a time. Entry placement should use multiple performance samples and a human escalation route. Advancement can be suggested from evidence but should not trap users behind camera hardware, disability, or one opaque score. Allow review and educator-directed placement. Advanced completion requires substantial authentic practice and independent review outside the app’s participation metrics.

Gallaudet’s [ASLPI overview](https://gallaudet.edu/aslpi/) provides an example of a separate language evaluation process. Learners seeking official or professional evidence of proficiency should use the assessment appropriate to their purpose. Interpreting is a separate professional skill and training path.

## Data contract and validation

The unit authoring source is `lib/data/program_blueprints.dart`. It contains 72 immutable const briefs with task, question, role, rubric, prerequisite, source, and approval metadata. `lib/data/curriculum.dart` compiles these into unmodifiable `CourseLevel`, `CourseUnit`, `Lesson`, and `LessonStep` collections while preserving all 54 original lesson IDs, titles, modes, and steps. The public `LessonMode`, `courseLevels`, `starterLesson`, and `allLessons` interfaces remain available. `CourseUnit` exposes objectives, vocabulary targets, grammar and culture focus, prerequisites, rubric, sources, scenario, and review/media status. `Lesson` exposes `activityKind`, `objectives`, `requiresReferenceVideo`, and stable `conceptIds` for separate review scheduling. Generic camera prompt text is not treated as a lexical sign identifier.

New lesson IDs combine the stable unit ID and activity kind. The original starter remains `a-first-hello`. Titles can change without invalidating storage keys, but a material change to assessed content requires a future explicit content version rather than silently retaining mastery. The 72-unit prerequisite sequence is proposed pedagogy, not a validated placement algorithm or a claim that a completed checkbox establishes readiness.

This version has six bands, exactly 12 units per band, exactly eight activity kinds per unit, unique IDs throughout, valid acyclic prerequisites, nonempty instructions and prompts, positive durations and XP, and valid concept-answer indexes. Every unit has at least two outcomes, five concept or vocabulary targets, three rubric criteria, and two primary-source URLs. The content stays separate from presentation and camera services so it can be migrated into a reviewed, localized content pipeline. English vocabulary labels are learning targets, not canonical glosses or one-to-one translations. Localization must preserve meaning and must not mechanically convert signed examples into a different sign language.

Sources were consulted on 2026-09-05. The source list supports the pedagogical principles above; it is not a claim that the demo lessons have been formally reviewed, licensed from, or approved by those organizations.
